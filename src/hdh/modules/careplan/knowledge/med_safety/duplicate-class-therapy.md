Two drugs from the same class rarely add benefit and reliably add risk. The
second agent brings the same adverse-effect profile as the first, so the
dose-dependent harms accumulate while the therapeutic effect plateaus.

It is usually an accident of accumulation rather than a decision. One drug
is started in clinic, another added in hospital or by a different service,
and neither prescriber sees the whole list — which is why duplication
appears most often in patients with several problems and several
prescribers, exactly the group least able to tolerate it.

Some duplications are intentional and should not be disturbed: dual
antiplatelet therapy after a stent, or a scheduled changeover where the old
drug has not yet been stopped. The distinction is whether a reason is
recorded. A duplication nobody can explain is the one to raise.

The review question is which agent to keep, not whether to stop both, and
whether the second was intended as a replacement that was never completed.
