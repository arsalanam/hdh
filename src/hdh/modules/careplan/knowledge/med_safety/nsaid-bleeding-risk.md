NSAIDs raise bleeding risk by two separate mechanisms, and both matter when
an anticoagulant is already prescribed. They inhibit platelet aggregation,
so clot formation is impaired at the same time as the coagulation cascade
is being suppressed by the other drug. They also injure the gastric mucosa
directly, which supplies a site for bleeding to start.

The combination is therefore worse than the sum of the two drugs read
separately, which is why a medication list that looks unremarkable drug by
drug can still be the problem. Neither agent is contraindicated on its own,
and a review that considers each in isolation will find nothing.

Where both are on the list, the questions are usually whether the NSAID is
still needed at all, whether paracetamol or a topical NSAID would answer
the same symptom, and — if the oral NSAID must continue — whether gastric
protection is in place and over what period the combination is intended to
run. An NSAID begun for a short course and never stopped is the common
version of this problem.
