Whether a patient lives alone changes how a medication risk should be
weighed, not merely how it should be counselled. Any adverse effect whose
harm depends on someone noticing it — hypoglycaemia, sedation, postural
hypotension, a fall — is more dangerous without a person in the house.

The practical consequences are usually small and concrete: a monitoring
arrangement that alerts somebody other than the patient, a simplified
regimen with fewer opportunities to double-dose, packaging that makes a
missed dose visible, and a check that the patient can act on a warning
without help.

Recording social isolation as a health concern in its own right, rather
than as background, is what allows an intervention to be attached to it
and its effect to be reviewed.
